# Research Brief: A Song of Ice and Fire, Fire & Blood, Dunk & Egg and the HBO shows

Purpose: raw material for designing a single-player STORY MODE of a Minecraft-like voxel game in which the player is born, is bonded to a dragon, grows up with it and builds things together with it.

Everything below is paraphrased in my own words from public summaries (see Sources). Where books and TV disagree I say so. Where only secondary sources were available (the main fan wiki blocked automated access) I flag the claim as "per secondary sources".

---

## 1. Plot summaries, arc by arc

### 1.1 The novels of A Song of Ice and Fire

**A Game of Thrones (1996).** King Robert makes his old friend Ned Stark Hand of the King after the previous Hand dies under suspicious circumstances. Ned discovers that the royal children are not Robert's but the product of the queen's affair with her twin brother, and that the Lannisters will kill to hide it. His son Bran is pushed from a tower for seeing too much. Robert dies, Ned tries to block the boy-king Joffrey, is betrayed by the cunning councillor Littlefinger and is executed. Robb Stark is proclaimed King in the North. On the Wall, Jon Snow takes the black and learns that the dead are walking again. Overseas, the exiled Daenerys is sold in marriage to the warlord Khal Drogo, loses him and her unborn child to a botched blood ritual, and walks into his funeral pyre with three petrified eggs. She walks out alive with three hatched dragons. The ending announces that magic is returning.

**A Clash of Kings (1999).** The realm splinters into the War of the Five Kings: Joffrey, Robb, Balon Greyjoy, Renly and Stannis. Stannis's red priestess kills Renly with a shadow. Tyrion, running the capital as acting Hand, holds off Stannis at the Battle of the Blackwater with wildfire and a giant chain, then is saved by his father's arrival, and his reward is to be shoved aside. Theon seizes Winterfell and loses it again to a far worse enemy. Jon scouts beyond the Wall. In Qarth, Daenerys's young dragons make her famous and hunted, and one burns down the sorcerers' House of the Undying. The book ends with the war unresolved and the dragons still small.

**A Storm of Swords (2000).** Widely considered the peak. Robb breaks his marriage promise to House Frey and the Red Wedding massacres him, his mother and most of his army. Joffrey is poisoned at his own wedding, Tyrion is framed, loses a trial by combat when his champion is killed, and escapes by murdering his father. Daenerys trades one dragon for an army of slave soldiers, then frees them, and takes the slave city of Meereen, deciding to stay and rule rather than march home. At the Wall, Jon survives a wildling assault and is elected Lord Commander. Sansa escapes the capital, Arya reaches Braavos, and a dead Catelyn returns as a vengeful revenant.

**A Feast for Crows (2005).** Set mostly in the south, and deliberately quieter. Cersei's regency rots from within: she gives power to flatterers, defaults on the debt to the Iron Bank, revives the militant faith and is arrested by the fanatics she empowered. Jaime, losing faith in her, drifts. Brienne searches for Sansa. Euron Greyjoy wins the Iron Islands by promising dragons. Dorne's plots fall apart. Arya trains with the Faceless Men and is blinded. Sam reaches the Citadel. The book is a study of what happens after a war: hunger, opportunists, and broken institutions.

**A Dance with Dragons (2011).** Runs alongside the fourth book and then goes past it. Daenerys rules Meereen, bans slavery and discovers that ruling is harder than conquering; she locks two dragons in a vault after one kills a child. Drogon, free and wild, finally carries her off after a riot in the fighting pit. A would-be suitor, Quentyn Martell, dies trying to steal a dragon, and the remaining dragons are released. Jon sets the Watch on a risky peace with the wildlings and is stabbed by his own men. Tyrion crosses the east and persuades a young pretender to invade Westeros right now. The ending is a bundle of cliffhangers; the series is still unfinished.

### 1.2 Fire & Blood (2018)

A faux-history of House Targaryen written by an in-world scholar who relies on conflicting sources, so the reader is invited to doubt it. It covers, in order:

- **Aegon's Conquest.** A minor Valyrian family on a rocky island sets out to take a continent with three dragons and two sister-wives, burns a castle, and unites six of the Seven Kingdoms (Dorne holds out).
- **The sons of the Conqueror.** A weak king, then a cruel one who builds the first dragon-housing, then a collapse into civil strife.
- **Jaehaerys and Alysanne.** A long, wise, productive reign by a king and his sister-queen: roads, laws, a stable realm. Their success breeds a succession puzzle because too many heirs die and too many remain.
- **Viserys I and the Dying of the Dragons.** A king names his daughter heir, remarries, and lets two factions of the family form. After his death comes the Dance of the Dragons, a civil war that kills most of the dragons in the world.
- **Aftermath.** A child king and a council of regents govern a bruised kingdom. The book stops there, mid-regency.

### 1.3 The Tales of Dunk and Egg (A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms)

Set roughly 90 years before the main novels. These are small, human-scale road stories.

- **The Hedge Knight.** Dunk, a poor and enormously tall wanderer, takes the gear of the old knight who trained him and goes to a tournament. He acquires a bald stable boy, Egg, as squire, strikes a prince who is bullying a puppeteer, and is forced into a trial of seven. His side wins, but the cost is lives, including a beloved prince. The boy's father then tells Dunk to keep training him.
- **The Sworn Sword.** Dunk serves an aged knight in a dispute over water rights with a young noblewoman. Diplomacy fails and he wins a duel in a river, nearly drowning. Old rebellion politics bubble up underneath.
- **The Mystery Knight.** At a wedding tournament, hidden rebels and a stolen dragon egg conspire. Egg's secret identity nearly slips and a rebel prince is captured.

The point of the novellas: this is what the high politics of the main saga look like from the ground. HBO adapted them as *A Knight of the Seven Kingdoms* (premiered January 2026, half-hour episodes, warmer and funnier than the other shows).

### 1.4 HBO: Game of Thrones (2011-2019, 8 seasons)

1. **S1.** Ned uncovers the secret and dies. Robb goes to war. Daenerys survives the pyre with three dragons.
2. **S2.** The War of the Five Kings. The Blackwater battle. Theon's betrayal. Daenerys wanders Qarth.
3. **S3.** The Red Wedding. Daenerys gains the slave army and is hailed as liberator.
4. **S4.** The Purple Wedding, Tyrion's trial, and the escape. Daenerys has to chain two of her dragons.
5. **S5.** Cersei and the Sparrows; Jon is killed; Stannis burns his daughter; Daenerys is captured by the Dothraki.
6. **S6.** Jon returns; the Battle of the Bastards; Cersei burns the sept with wildfire; Daenerys sails for Westeros with fleets and dragons.
7. **S7.** Daenerys lands at Dragonstone, wins with dragonfire at the Loot Train, and allies with Jon. The Night King kills a dragon with an ice spear and raises it.
8. **S8.** The Battle of Winterfell against the dead. Then the war against Cersei, in which Daenerys, after a series of losses, burns King's Landing. Jon kills her; Bran is made king; the North goes independent. The ending split fans sharply and the compressed pacing was widely criticised.

### 1.5 HBO: House of the Dragon (2022-)

1. **S1.** Viserys names Rhaenyra heir, then marries Alicent Hightower, whose father wants her son on the throne. Years pass; childhood friends become rival mothers. Rhaenyra's sons are claimed to be illegitimate. Aemond claims the giant Vhagar and loses an eye in a fight. Viserys dies, Aegon II is crowned in secret, and the season ends when Aemond's dragon kills Rhaenyra's young son Luke and his dragon. War is inevitable.
2. **S2.** The Greens hold the city; the Blacks hold Dragonstone. Rhaenys is killed at Rook's Rest. Aegon is burned and comatose; Aemond becomes regent. Rhaenyra recruits low-born "dragonseeds" who claim abandoned dragons. The season ends with mobilisation for the full war.
3. **S3 (2026).** Per Wikipedia's summary: the disastrous Gullet naval battle, Rhaenyra briefly holding the throne and hardening into a tyrant, the Tumbleton campaign and a nightmare of fire on a town, and an unresolved ending. A fourth and final season has been ordered.

## 2. The big themes

- **Succession and legitimacy.** Everything in these stories starts with the question of who has the right to the throne, and the answer is never clean: a deathbed claim, a rule of male preference broken, a bastard birth, a conquest by sword. A succession rule that is not obeyed is simply a war waiting for a trigger.
- **Family loyalty versus ambition.** Siblings and cousins love each other and destroy each other. The best versions are those in which both sides are sympathetic: two childhood friends whose children are pushed into war, or a brother who must choose between oath and bloodline.
- **Power's cost.** Every ruler pays: Daenerys loses her husband, child and eventually her humanity; Rhaenyra pays in sons; Ned pays for honor; Cersei pays for control. Winning makes people worse. Power gets used on the people it was meant to protect.
- **Duty versus desire.** Oaths contradict one another (loyal to a king, loyal to a house, loyal to justice), and any choice betrays something.
- **Prophecy and fate.** Visions and dreams steer characters, who then fulfil them by trying to avoid them. Prophecy is never a map; it is a source of dread and of self-justification.
- **Fire and ice.** The series title is literal: dragons (fire, power, conquest, the family) versus the cold dead from beyond the Wall (ice, extinction, an enemy that cares nothing for politics). Humans fight over a throne while the real threat marches. In the Targaryen backstory fire is itself a double-edged gift: it builds the dynasty and finally wrecks it.
- **The fall of a dynasty.** The Targaryens rise on three dragons and fall when the dragons die, killed first by their own infighting, then by neglect and fear. The slow decay of a bloodline's greatness is a theme repeated at small scale in every house.
- **Found family.** Dunk and Egg, the Night's Watch brothers, the Stark siblings scattered across the world, the people who gather around Daenerys. The warmest scenes in the saga are always between people who chose each other.
- **History is written by survivors.** Fire & Blood's unreliable narrators and the many rumours about dragon deaths teach that the past is a story told by those with an interest in it.

## 3. Dragon lore in depth

### 3.1 Origins: Valyria and the dragonlords

- Dragons are tied to the Valyrian Freehold, a civilisation on a peninsula ringed by volcanoes called the Fourteen Flames. Valyrian tradition says dragons began there; an eastern tradition says they were brought from a far dark land. The Valyrians learned to tame them using magic (a vanished people is credited as teachers) and made them weapons.
- The ruling class were the **dragonlords**: roughly forty families whose power rested on dragons, bloodlines, and Valyrian steel. Dragons let them crush rivals, build roads and fortresses and enslave neighbours.
- The control was partly magical: horns and binding spells were said to compel dragons. One surviving horn appears in the later novels as a plot device and as a weapon that burns its user.
- **The Doom.** About a century before the Conquest the volcanoes erupted and the whole Freehold, with its dragons and dragonlords, was destroyed in a single catastrophe. The ruin is now cursed and avoided.
- **The Targaryens survived** because a girl, Daenys the Dreamer, had a prophetic dream of the disaster, and her father moved the family to Dragonstone, an island fortress at the edge of the Freehold, twelve years before. They were the only dragonlord house to escape, with a handful of dragons and a number of eggs.

### 3.2 Eggs, hatching, bonding and naming

- **Eggs** are large, heavy, stone-hard, brightly coloured, and they do not hatch by ordinary means; in the main saga they are heirlooms, treasures and diplomatic gifts. Over the long era after the dragons die, they sit in vaults as symbols.
- **The cradle custom.** Per Fire & Blood summaries, a Targaryen princess began the habit of putting a dragon egg in the cradle of a newborn sibling; it became tradition that a royal child slept beside an egg, and the children so blessed nearly always bonded to the hatchling. An egg that did not hatch was read as a bad omen about the child's blood. The tradition continued as pure ritual after hatchings stopped.
- **Hatching** is triggered by closeness, heat and the child. In the main saga, Daenerys's three eggs hatch in a funeral fire that she enters herself, a moment treated as a rebirth and as proof that "the magic is back".
- **Bonding** is a one-to-one tie that lasts for life. A dragon usually accepts one rider at a time, and when the rider dies the dragon may become wild, remain unbonded, or in rare cases accept a new rider. A rider is chosen by the dragon rather than the other way round; a bond cannot be forced by command, only by acceptance.
- **Claiming a grown dragon** is lethal. In the Dance, bastards of the Valyrian bloodline are invited to try to claim unridden dragons; sources disagree on the exact tally, but about sixteen die and roughly three times as many are burned or maimed in a day. Success seems to depend on a mix of nerve, blood, calm, luck and sometimes a selfless act.
- **Names.** Dragons are given Valyrian names, often mythic or from the family's history, such as Balerion, Meraxes, Vhagar, Caraxes, Syrax, Meleys, Sunfyre, Silverwing, Vermithor. The Dothraki-flavoured set in the main saga honours the dead (Drogon, Rhaegal, Viserion for the husband and two brothers of their mother). The rider's choice of a name is part of claiming.

### 3.3 Growth, size and age

- Hatchlings are about the size of a house cat and grow fast; with enough food and space they can be ridden in roughly two years.
- **Dragons never stop growing** as long as they have food and room to roam; growth is limited by confinement and scarcity, not age. Dragons kept in cages, vaults or under a roof stay smaller. In the late Targaryen era, captive-bred dragons were visibly stunted and the last were small and misshapen.
- The record holders are the old free-flying dragons: Balerion the Black Dread, whose jaws could swallow a mammoth, Vhagar, who could swallow a horse and rider, and Meraxes. Balerion is the only dragon recorded to die of old age, at over 200 years.
- Colour carries no meaning; scale and fire colours vary and have no link to temperament or sex. Dragon bone is black, stronger than steel but flexible, and prized for weapons and jewellery.
- Biological details (sex, lifespan, mating) are left deliberately murky, and in-world scholars argue about them.

### 3.4 Temperament

- Dragons are neither pets nor steeds. They are wilful, proud, easily angered, and lethal. They obey a rider out of attachment, not obedience, and they sometimes act against orders (Vhagar kills a prince and his dragon when her rider is unable to restrain her; a dragon turns on Daenerys's would-be conqueror).
- Different dragons are visibly different characters: elegant Sunfyre, ancient and territorial Vhagar, battle-scarred Caraxes, gentle Syrax, moody Silverwing, fierce Vermithor, and wild unridden dragons living in volcanoes and eating sheep.
- Dragons respond to the emotional state of their riders; in the stories the bond is strongest between bonded pairs of children and hatchlings who grow up together.
- In captivity and under chains dragons grow more savage, as shown by Daenerys's locked-away pair.

### 3.5 How riders mount and fight

- Riders mount by climbing a dragon's neck and sit in a saddle fitted with straps and chains. Experienced riders can leap from dragon to dragon in mid-air.
- Dragons are used as strategic weapons: they scout, burn fleets, scatter armies, terrify garrisons into surrender, and break gates. A single large dragon can win a battle; three together, as at the Field of Fire, wiped out a coalition army.
- Dragons fight each other in aerial duels that are chaotic, intimate and usually fatal. Rider skill matters but the dragon's size and age matter more.
- The flying is part of identity; reaching adulthood in a Targaryen family means first flight.

### 3.6 Dragonfire and what it does to stone

- Dragonfire can melt stone, fuse sand into glass, and turn steel soft. The classic example is **Harrenhal**: the biggest castle in Westeros, built by a king who boasted that its walls were dragon-proof, was set aflame in a single evening by Balerion, and its towers melted into the grotesque, drooping shapes that remain today. The king and all his sons died within.
- Castles are rendered useless as defences. Walls, moats and iron gates matter little. This changes the whole logic of war: fortifications are replaced by anti-air defences (scorpions, archers), by fear, and by the hope that the dragon will be busy elsewhere.
- Fire also destroys without discrimination: the Tumbleton scene in the Dance and the burning of the capital in the TV finale both show that dragons are weapons of terror whose victims are ordinary people.

### 3.8 The Dragonpit

- A vast domed structure on a hill in King's Landing, built by the cruel king Maegor to keep dragons close. It was large enough for dozens of knights to enter side by side, and a symbol of dynastic power.
- Because it kept dragons confined, later dragons grew small, weak and sullen, and the building itself is part of the story of decline.
- During the Dance a mob incited by a street preacher storms it and kills most of the dragons inside, plus another dragon that swoops down to help. The Targaryens never recover their military monopoly.
- After the war the Pit is rebuilt, but the dragons it was meant for are mostly gone. A modern-era ruin of a hall for animals that will not return.

### 3.9 The Dance of the Dragons

- **Cause.** Viserys picks his daughter Rhaenyra as heir; he then remarries and has sons. The Hightower faction claims he named the elder son on his deathbed. Two courts, each with dragons, each certain it is right.
- **Shape.** The two sides recruit lords, hire fleets, buy loyalties. Dragons strike at fleets (Gullet), forts, and each other; riders die in duels (above the Gods Eye); a town is burned from the air (Tumbleton). Rhaenyra takes the capital briefly, then loses it, and is eventually fed to Aegon II's dragon in front of her son. Aegon II dies shortly after, apparently poisoned. Rhaenyra's boy son becomes king.
- **Result.** Out of about twenty dragons at the beginning, only a few remain by the end. The dynasty has won its war and lost its power.

### 3.10 Why the dragons died out

Reasons in the sources, none of them confirmed as the single cause:

1. **War losses**: most died in the Dance, in aerial duels, in sieges, from scorpions.
2. **The Dragonpit riot**: a mob killed several at once.
3. **Captivity and stunted growth**: the confined generation was smaller, sicker and harder to breed; the last hatchlings were deformed.
4. **No more hatching**: eggs stopped hatching, for reasons nobody knows.
5. **Neglect and fear**: the child-king Aegon III, who had watched a dragon devour his mother, may have been terrified of them; rumour says the last dragon was poisoned. He came to be nicknamed "Dragonbane".
6. **Magic fading**: some in-world believers hold that the magic of the world left when the dragons did, and that winters grew longer and crueller afterwards.

Late Targaryen kings made disastrous attempts to revive them. One tried to hatch five eggs with a great fire that burned a castle and killed many (the same family tragedy that touches Dunk's squire). Another sat on an egg for a day and a night. Nothing worked until the one princess who walked into a funeral fire.

### 3.11 Weak points and dragonbane

- **The eye** is the repeated weak point: Meraxes by a bolt, Vhagar by a sword, and Tessarion by a mercy killing with arrows. Heavy scorpion bolts (giant crossbows) are the standard anti-dragon weapon, and are used from ships and walls. The belly and the throat are also vulnerable per most accounts.
- **Other dragons** are the real danger: most dragon deaths are by a larger dragon.
- **Dragonglass** (obsidian) is associated with killing the ice-people, not dragons. Dragon bone is black, a bit like it.
- **Valyrian steel** is a legendary metal that holds an edge and is associated with dragonlords' swords.
- **Hunger, captivity, poison, and infection** are less glamorous killers; an infected burn killed a golden dragon over weeks.
- **Ice-magic** can kill: the Night King's spear.

### 3.12 Dragon eggs as symbols

- **Legitimacy and blood.** A hatching egg proves you are a true dragon. A dead egg is an omen.
- **Hope.** Stone eggs inherited by a family that cannot hatch them are a symbol of what has been lost; a hatched egg is a return of wonder.
- **Wealth and diplomacy.** Eggs are given as wedding presents and sought by rivals; they are among the most valuable objects on earth.
- **Trial by fire.** A person who walks into a fire with an egg and walks out is reborn.
- **Heirlooms and burden.** The family keeps them as "dead" objects, and the egg in a baby's cradle quietly says, "you are meant to be more than human".

## 4. Character archetypes and relationships that make it work

- **The bonded rider.** A person whose identity is intertwined with a creature, such as the orphaned princess who finds in her dragon the only family that stays. The dragon is their dependable love and their uncontrollable threat. When the creature does something terrible, the person must answer for it. (Daenerys with Drogon; Rhaenyra with Syrax; Aemond's overweening Vhagar.)
- **The rival sibling / the sibling-turned-enemy.** Two children raised together, each convinced the other is the threat, each defended by those with something to gain. Their tragedy is that either one alone would have been loved.
- **The mentor.** The older figure who shapes the youngster through a modest example: a hedge knight who is not wise or powerful but is kind; an old maester who sacrifices himself; a grandfather king who teaches duty. The mentor is usually gone by the midpoint, and the player has to carry on the lesson.
- **The usurper.** The person who takes the throne by law, force or cleverness. Interesting because they are not simply evil: they think they are protecting the realm.
- **The loyal knight.** Sworn to a person or a cause even when it is hopeless; their loyalty is tested by a choice between a vow and the right thing. Often the tragic casualty.
- **The cunning councillor.** The whisperer, spymaster or Hand who benefits from chaos and steers the monarch; the player never quite knows whose side they are on.
- **The conflicted queen-mother.** A parent who loves her children and drives them into a war.
- **The wild thing.** The unridden dragon on the volcano: beautiful, dangerous, and a reminder of what the bonded dragon was before.
- **The found-family squire.** A small, observant, secretly noble child who follows a decent adult, as Egg follows Dunk.

Relationship patterns that work:

- A **bond between unequals**: a small child and a huge animal; a poor knight and a disguised prince.
- A **friendship destroyed by politics**: childhood closeness that turns to enmity as the adults around them sort their interests.
- A **parent/child inversion**: a child who must protect the adult.
- A **grief-driven decision**: a character chooses cruelty after loss.

## 5. Story-structure patterns worth borrowing for a game

### 5.1 How tension escalates

1. **Start small, local and intimate**: a birth, a hearth, an egg in a cradle. The big politics are rumours at the edges.
2. **Establish the rules early**: dragons grow, eggs hatch, fire melts stone. Those rules then pay off later.
3. **Plant a looming threat that nobody takes seriously**: cold winds beyond a wall, a dying dynasty, an unnamed enemy.
4. **Lock in a tripwire**: a promise or an oath that will be broken (a marriage pact, a deathbed word, a stolen egg).
5. **Pull the rug at the midpoint**: a mentor dies, a trusted ally betrays, a safe place burns. Ned's execution at the end of book one is the model.
6. **Let consequences accrue**: choices made early echo hundreds of hours later.
7. **Escalate by raising the cost of victory**, not just the number of enemies.

### 5.2 Set-piece moments (candidates for a dragon-bonding game)

- **The first hatching**: an egg warms, cracks, and the creature chooses you. A single quiet beat, with no combat.
- **The first flight**: a short unfettered moment of freedom and scale; the world opens up.
- **The burning of a fortress**: the dragon's power on display, with lasting visual change to the world (melted, glassy, twisted blocks).
- **The siege defended by a dragon**: the first time the player's home is attacked and the dragon must choose between defence and fury.
- **The wild dragon encounter**: a creature on a volcano, a taming-or-respecting challenge.
- **The aerial duel**: dragon vs. bigger dragon, rider vs. rider.
- **The funeral pyre**: an ending of a mentor's life, the egg that hatches in the fire.
- **The Dragonpit moment**: the player's own home is turned against the dragon; a mob, a decree, a captured dragon in chains.
- **The reunion**: after separation, the bond restored.

### 5.3 Tragic beats

- **Someone close dies unfairly and early** (the mentor, the parent, the first friend).
- **The dragon is hurt or taken**, and the player must save it.
- **A beloved place is destroyed** (a village the player built).
- **A hard choice with no right answer**: loyalty against love, chaining a dragon against letting it run wild.
- **The consequence of power**: the player's own dragon harms someone innocent, and the world responds.
- **A slow decline**: later, the old dragons are no longer as large as the legends.

### 5.4 Rewards

- **Visible growth**: the dragon changes size, silhouette and abilities as the player ages, and the player can see the difference (cat-sized hatchling, rideable juvenile, castle-sized adult).
- **Unlocks tied to the bond**: flight, fire-breath that reshapes blocks, a dragon that carries cargo or fetches ore.
- **Legacy items**: a dragon-bone tool, an heirloom egg, a banner.
- **Lore and revelations**: the history of Valyria and what happened to the dragons, in found artefacts and ruins.
- **Found family**: companions who join the settlement.
- **A final choice** that echoes the saga's central question: what do you do with overwhelming power?

### 5.5 Design takeaways for a voxel story mode

1. **Let the dragon's size depend on how you treat it**: free flight and good feeding grow it; confinement stunts it. This turns a rule from the lore into gameplay (a shelter, a nest, an open sky).
2. **Build the Dragonpit as a player choice**: building a safe pen gives short-term safety and long-term cost.
3. **Dragonfire as terraforming**: melting stone to glass, scorching walls into twisted shapes that remain, and ruined castles that the player can rebuild.
4. **A personal succession story**: the player is born into a house whose heirs argue over the throne; the siblings' relationships are the emotional core, with a rivalry that the player can try to heal or let fester.
5. **Use the mentor-squire pair as the quiet counterweight**: a gentle companion thread that stays warm while the main plot darkens.
6. **Fire versus ice as a two-act spine**: Act 1 and 2 warm (growing up, building), Act 3 cold (the winter that the dragons were meant to hold back).
7. **Keep the wild dragons**: the world should have unbonded dragons that the player can meet, respect, or leave alone; they make the bonded one feel chosen.
8. **Let the history be unreliable**: lore as in-world books with conflicting accounts, so players piece together truth.
9. **The last dragon**: an ending in which the choice is how to preserve the species, and whether power should be passed on.
